# A Convolutional Neural Network Model for Battery Capacity Fade Curve Prediction using Early Life Data

Saurabh Saxena<sup>1\*</sup>, Logan Ward<sup>2</sup>, Joseph Kubal<sup>3</sup>, Wenquan Lu<sup>3</sup>, Susan Babinec<sup>4</sup>, and Noah Paulson<sup>1</sup>

<sup>1</sup>Applied Materials Division, Argonne National Laboratory, Lemont, IL 60439 USA

<sup>2</sup>Data Science and Learning Division, Argonne National Laboratory, Lemont, IL 60439 USA

<sup>3</sup>Chemical Sciences and Engineering Division, Argonne National Laboratory, Lemont, IL 60439 USA

<sup>4</sup>Argonne Collaborative Center for Energy Storage Science (ACCESS), Argonne National Laboratory, Lemont, IL 60439 USA

## Abstract

Early prediction of battery performance degradation trends can facilitate research of new materials and cell designs, rapid deployment of batteries in real-world applications, timely replacement of batteries in critical applications, and even the secondary use market. In this study, we design a convolutional neural network model to predict the entire battery capacity fade curve – a critical indicator of battery performance degradation – using first 100 cycles of data (\~ three weeks of testing). We use the discharge voltage-capacity curves as input to the model and automate the feature extraction process through the convolutional layers of the network. Our approach can predict the per cycle capacity fade rate and rollover cycle (knee point) in the capacity fade curve, which indicate the onset of rapid capacity decay. On the publicly available graphite/ $\mathrm { \dot { \Omega } i F e P O _ { 4 } }$ battery dataset, optimized networks predict the capacity fade curves, rollover cycle, and end of life with 3.7% (worst-case), 19%, and 17% mean absolute percentage errors, respectively.

Keywords: Lithium-ion battery; capacity fade; state of health; end of life; machine learning

## 1. Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries continue to penetrate new markets as their technology improves and cost declines. However, their performance degrades over the operational life and, in some cases, shows a rapid decline after the "knee point".

This degradation occurs as a result of multiple complex mechanisms including electrode-electrolyte interfacial sidereactions, lithium plating, structural degradation of electrode materials, and decomposition of the electrolyte [1]. Thus, battery performance degradation characterization through extensive testing and the development of health prediction algorithms have been at the forefront of battery research.

Battery capacity, the amount of charge (Ampere-hours) that the battery can store, is considered a lead indicator of battery performance and health. Usually, battery end of life (EOL) is defined as the time or number of cycles when the battery capacity falls below a predefined percentage (80% in many applications) of its initial/nominal value. Determination of the entire battery capacity fade trend not only serves to estimate the battery EOL but also facilitates the qualification of battery for the field usage. However, battery testing to determine capacity fade trend can involve hundreds to thousands of charge discharge cycling operations and can take months to years – an issue which significantly constraints the battery-powered product development cycle. Hence, there is a need to develop tools for battery capacity fade trend prediction using limited testing data generated within short time frames. Many researchers have developed a variety of models to estimate and predict the battery capacity fade trend. These models can be divided into two types: first-principle electrochemical models and data-driven models based on battery (capacity, voltage, current, and/or temperature) measurements.

Electrochemical approaches combine the conventional pseudo two dimensional (P2D) model [2], [3] or single particle model [4] with specific degradation mechanism models to predict battery capacity fade trend. They deploy partial differential equations to describe physical phenomena such as mass transport, charge transfer, thermodynamics, and chemical kinetics [5]–[7]. While electrochemical models are a promising route to predict battery capacity fade trends, gaps in the knowledge of degradation mechanisms coupled with the difficultly in identifying design and material specific parameters in the models limit their widespread use.

Data-driven approaches which are derived directly from the battery measurement data typically falls into two categories: approaches relying solely on the capacity series data as input and approaches utilizing a more diverse input data of voltage, current, and/or temperature in addition to the capacity. Capacity-based approaches use power, exponential [8], and double-exponential [9] functions to describe the capacity fade. These models [8], [9] deploy recursive Bayesian estimation methods, such as particle filters, to incorporate uncertainty in predictions and to update the model parameters iteratively as more data becomes available. There have also been efforts to use machine learning (ML) models including advanced neural networks [10]–[12] to capture the capacity fade trend over cycles.

While the problem of capacity fade prediction has been pursued in the past by many researchers, when it comes to predicting the entire capacity fade trend using just first 100 or lesser cycles of dataset as model input, the literature has been very limited and only recently researchers have started to pursue this problem. Li et al. [11] have utilized a capacity-based approach and demonstrated the earliest prediction of the entire capacity fade trend with a worst-case mean error of 4.2% using input data up to the first 100 cycles – just \~7% of the average life of the batteries used in the study. They develop a recurrent neural network-based sequence-to-sequence (RNN-S2S) model and use a dataset of nominally identical 47 graphite/NMC cells cycled under the same operating conditions for the model training (42 cells) and evaluation (5 cells). Capacity-based approaches can be advanced further by including other battery measurements such as voltage and current as input, allowing the ML models to learn more complex and generalizable battery degradation patterns.

Recently, data-driven ML models utilizing battery voltage, current, temperature, and/or capacity data have been successfully used for battery EOL [13]–[16], remaining useful life (RUL) [17], and rollover cycle (knee point) prediction [18] using the first 100 or fewer cycles of data. Three studies [16], [19], [20] have also shown the prediction of capacity fade curves using the first 100 or fewer cycles of data. Strange and dos Reis [16] use two different 9-layer one-dimensional convolutional neural networks (1D - CNN) to jointly predict “time (cycles) to” (cycles to knee-onset, cycles to knee point, and RUL) and “capacity” (capacity at knee-onset, capacity at knee-point) quantities to create the capacity fade curves. They use 165 graphite $\mathrm { / L i F e P O _ { 4 } }$ cells from the cycling datasets in [15], [21] and use a single cycle (somewhere within first 100 cycles) of voltage, current, and state of charge data as input to their models. Herring et al. [19] use data and featurization from first 100 cycles [15] and develop multi-task linear models to predict cycles to reach 95%, 92%, 89%, 86%, 83% and 80% of nominal capacity, which then used to create the capacity fade curves. Similarly, using the features and the dataset from [15], Liu et al. [20] present an elastic net-based approach to estimate the parameters of an empirical power function which describe the capacity fade curves beyond 100 cycles.

The qualitative comparison of model predictions and actual capacity fade curves presented in [16], [19], [20] shows that early prediction of capacity fade curve still remains a challenge and that more advanced ML models with better features could yield significant benefits. Early prediction is essential for the rapid deployment and timely replacement of batteries in real-world applications and can enable secondary usage market and accelerated novel battery materials and cell design developments. Thus, to advance the early prediction capabilities, we investigate deep learning models of 2 dimensional (2D) CNNs, which can automatically generate relevant spatial features capturing the dynamics of battery measurements not just within single cycle but also over multiple cycles for capacity fade curve prediction.

In this study, we design a single 2D-CNN model with 6 layers to predict the entire battery capacity fade trend up to 80% of the nominal capacity using discharge curves from all of the first 100 cycles. We use a public dataset [15], [21] of 178 graphite $\mathrm { L i F e P O _ { 4 } }$ cells cycled under different fast charging conditions and a common discharge condition for model training (108 cells – training), selection (35 cells- validation), and evaluation (35 cell – test). We discuss the process and implications of hyperparameters and loss function selection. Lastly, we quantify and present the mean absolute error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) for capacity fade curve, rollover cycle, and EOL predictions on the three datasets. Our modeling approach allows the predictions of per cycle capacity fade rate and rollover cycle as inherent parameters of a bilinear equation which we use to describe the capacity fade trend.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows: Section 2 briefly describes the Severson et al. [15] and Attia et al. [21] datasets used in this study. Section 3 presents a capacity fade trend parametrization approach that we use to describe the entire capacity fade trend. Section 4 provides the design analysis of the convolutional neural network architecture. Section 5 discusses the hyperparameters and loss function selection process with their implications on the model prediction results followed by the conclusions in Section 6.

## 2. Experimental Battery Dataset

We utilize the commercial graphite $\mathrm { L i F e P O _ { 4 } }$ cylindrical battery (A123 Systems) cycling dataset from Severson et al. [15] and Attia et al. [21] which has a total of 178 batteries<sup>†</sup> with test cycles ranging from 147 cycles to 2236 cycles. The cells have a nominal capacity of 1.1 Ah and a nominal voltage of 3.3 V. The charge-discharge cycling tests were conducted at a constant ambient temperature of $3 0 ~ ^ { \mathrm { o } } \mathrm { C } .$ Cells were charged with different fast-charging protocols but discharged using identical protocols. The charge protocol included a constant current – constant voltage (CC-CV) discharge at 4 C to 2.0 V with a current cutoff of C/50 (C/20 in [21]). Batteries were fast-charged with one or multiple CC steps up to 80% SOC followed by 1C CC-CV charge profile up to 3.6 V with a C/50 (C/20 in [21]) charge cutoff. For more details on the different charge protocols please refer to Severson et al. [15] and Attia et al. [21].

The discharge curves were replaced for the incomplete/missing cycles with those of the next available cycle.

## 3. Capacity Fade Curve Parametrization

Battery capacity fade trends serve as the final output of the developed ML model, but their length varies from cell to cell - as can be seen in Fig. 1a. Thus, we represent the capacity fade curves using a four-parameter bilinear equation to achieve a consistent ML model output size across different cells. The four parameters are: initial slope $\mathrm { ( m _ { o } ) }$ , rollover cycle $( \mathrm { N _ { k } } )$ , rollover width (δ), and failure slope (m<sub>f</sub>). The premise of this equation is that the slope of the capacity fade trend changes from an initial value to a final (failure) value after the rollover cycle (knee point). Hence it can capture the typical lithium-ion degradation characterized by a slow gradual capacity fade followed with a rapid decline. The rollover width controls the width of the transition window between the initial and failure slope. This transition (γ) has been modeled in the form of a sigmoid function as per the equation below:

$$
\gamma = \frac {1}{1 + e ^ {- (n - N _ {k}) / \delta}}\tag{1}
$$

$$
\frac {d q}{d n} = m _ {o} + (m _ {f} - m _ {o}) * \gamma\tag{2}
$$

Where, q represents the discharge capacity “loss” and n represents the number of cycles. Using the differential equation (2), a relationship between Q and n can be obtained as follows:

$$
q = m _ {o} * n + \left(m _ {f} - m _ {o}\right) * \delta * \ln \left(e ^ {n / \delta} + e ^ {N _ {k} / \delta}\right) + C\tag{3}
$$

where, C is a constant due to integral of Equation (2). Value of C can be determined by considering zero capacity loss $( \mathsf { q } = 0 )$ at cycle zero $( \mathbf { n } = 0 )$ in the Equation (3).

$$
0 = m _ {o} * 0 + \left(m _ {f} - m _ {o}\right) * \delta * \ln \left(e ^ {0 / \delta} + e ^ {N _ {k} / \delta}\right) + C
$$

$$
C = - \left(m _ {f} - m _ {o}\right) * \delta * \ln \left(1 + e ^ {N _ {k} / \delta}\right)\tag{4}
$$

$$
q = m _ {o} * n + (m _ {f} - m _ {o}) * \delta * \ln \left(e ^ {n / \delta} + e ^ {N _ {k} / \delta}\right) - (m _ {f} - m _ {o}) * \delta * \ln \left(1 + e ^ {N _ {k} / \delta}\right)
$$

$$
q = m _ {o} * n + (m _ {f} - m _ {o}) * \delta * \ln \left(\frac {e ^ {n / \delta} + e ^ {N _ {k / \delta}}}{1 + e ^ {N _ {k / \delta}}}\right)\tag{5}
$$

Equation (5) was used to fit the capacity fade trends to obtain the four parameters $( m _ { o } , N _ { k } , \delta , m _ { f } )$ for all the cells. A non-gradient based differential evolution optimization algorithm [22] provided equation parameters with MAE as the objective function to minimize. MAE is calculated between actual capacity fade curves and Equation (5) fitted curves at every 10 cycles - given that our interest lies in the long-term trend rather than the short-term capacity variations.

Differential evolution is a metaheuristic method which can search a large space of candidate solutions and as such we kept the value of the rollover width, $\delta ,$ constant as 50 for all the trends reducing the number of unknown parameters to three. This value of $\delta$ minimizes the mean and maximum absolute errors for training cells (108 cells) as a function of rollover width (Supplementary Fig. S1). Rollover width and rollover cycle together affect the transition between initial and final slope. A constant δ helped in better aligning the rollover cycle obtained by differential evolution with the one observed “visually” on the battery capacity fade curves. A constant δ will also reduce the number of parameters to be predicted by the neural network thus reducing the number of weights in the network. We put bounds on the search space for the three parameters to ensure that they follow the physical meaning assigned to them - they are (-1e-3, 0), (0, 3000), and (-2e-2, 0) for parameters $m _ { o } ,$ $N _ { k } ,$ m<sub>f,</sub> respectively.

Fig. 2 shows the histograms of initial slope, rollover cycle, and failure slope for the training cells. The distributions of parameters closely match the Cauchy, Rayleigh, and lognormal distributions, respectively. We selected these distributions based on the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with 0.05 significance level. We multiply the failure slope data by -1 to make it positive to match with the positive range of the lognormal distribution and then fit the respective distributions for all three parameters and obtain the distributions parameters (Equation (6)). Fitting the distributions to these three parameters allows us to represent parameters in the form of cumulative distribution functions (CDF) and bring them the same range of 0 to 1. A same range for all three parameters is useful for the stability and effective training of the neural network.

$$
f (m _ {o} | \gamma = - 4. 5 3 \times 1 0 ^ {- 5}, \lambda = 1. 4 7 \times 1 0 ^ {- 5}) = \frac {1}{\pi \lambda \left(1 + \left(\frac {m _ {o} - \gamma}{\lambda}\right) ^ {2}\right)}
$$

$$
f (N _ {k} | \gamma = 2 0 4. 1 5, \lambda = 3 3 5. 5 9) = \left(\frac {N _ {k} - \gamma}{\lambda^ {2}}\right) e x p \left(- \frac {(N _ {k} - \gamma) ^ {2}}{2 \lambda^ {2}}\right)\tag{6}
$$

$$
f \left(m _ {f} \mid \sigma = 1. 1 4, \gamma = 6. 5 9 \times 1 0 ^ {- 5}, \lambda = 1. 4 3 \times 1 0 ^ {- 3}\right) = \frac {1}{\sigma (m _ {f} - \gamma) \sqrt {2 \pi}} e x p \left(- \frac {\log^ {2} \left(\frac {m _ {f} - \gamma}{\lambda}\right)}{2 \sigma^ {2}}\right)
$$

## 4. Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) Architecture

Convolutional neural networks (CNN) have become a popular ML modeling tool due to their abilities to automate the feature engineering process, a critical step for building robust and accurate ML models, and to incorporate knowledge about the invariances of shapes or signals by using local connection patterns [23]. Compared to fully connected neural networks, convolutional networks can reduce the number of trainable weights and capture the topology of input. In convolutional layers, features are created by a fixed-size weight window (kernel/filter) sliding over the entire input (or previous layer) similar to the edge-detection filters common in computer vision. The entire process of feature extraction and classification/regression in CNNs is optimized jointly using backpropagation. A detailed description of the theory of the CNN is beyond the scope of this work and this section only discusses the architecture and learning process of the proposed CNN model.

Severson et al. [15] showed that the features manually extracted from the discharge curves of first 100 cycles could be used to predict the end of life of batteries. In this study, we use convolutional layers to capture the local patterns in discharge curves and to automate and scale up the process of feature extraction for capacity fade curve prediction. We use the discharge curves (Q vs V) of the first 100 cycles as raw input data to the CNN model. Selection of the first 100 cycles was motivated by the findings in [15] and the real-world requirement of achieving a trade-off between reducing the battery qualification time and accurately predicting the battery degradation trend and EOL. The discharge curves are arranged for a specific cell (Fig. 3a) as a $1 0 0 \times 1 0 0$ matrix representing 100 cycles vs capacity values at 100 equidistant terminal voltage values between 2.0V and 3.5V (Fig. 3b). The input matrix is standardized using the z-score method before feeding it into the CNN model input. The matrix can also be represented as an image colormap (Fig. 3c) to establish the equivalence with the image recognition application of CNN.

The proposed CNN model consists of four distinct types of layers: convolutional layers, batch normalization layers, max pooling layers, and fully connected layers. Convolutional layers identify local features from spatially nearby data in the input matrix (or previous layers) using linear 2-D filters (kernels). We apply batch normalization (BatchNorm) [24] to stabilize the distribution (over a minibatch) of inputs to a given network layer during training and for a more predictive and stable behavior of the gradients, allowing for faster training [25]. BatchNorm normalizes each scalar feature independently by setting the mean to be zero and the variance to be 1, which is then further scaled and shifted using trainable parameters (α, β) to represent the identity transform [24].

$$
y = \frac {x - E [ x ]}{\sqrt {V a r (x) + \epsilon}} * \alpha + \beta , \qquad \epsilon = 1 \times 1 0 ^ {- 5}\tag{7}
$$

Max pooling layers are subsampling layers that preserve the maximum value of features in a local region of input matrix. This subsampling step is useful in reducing the resolution of the feature map and achieving invariance to shifts and distortions in the feature maps [23]. The generated feature map after all the convolutional and max-pooling operations is flattened into a 1-D vector and fed to fully connected layers to predict cumulative distribution function (CDF) values of the 3 parameters (initial slope, rollover cycle, and failure slope) of the bilinear equation.

Fig. 3d shows the CNN architecture without the batch normalization layers considering they only transform the data without affecting its shape or size. The proposed CNN consists of four (2-D) convolutional layers, five (four 2-D, one 1-D) batch normalization layers, two (2-D) max pooling layers, and two fully connected layers (one hidden layer and one output layer). Table 1 lists the hyperparameters of these layers. There are 42899 tunable parameters in the network during training. Square kernels of sizes are $5 \times 5$ and $3 \times 3$ for convolutional layers and $2 \times 2$ for max pooling layers following the recommendations from the well-known image classification CNNs: AlexNet [26] and VGG [27]. We use a larger non-overlapping stride of 5 only in the first convolutional layer to reduce the data size from $1 0 0 \times 1 0 0$ to $2 0 \times 2 0 ;$ ; the per battery degradation characteristics, Q values change in approximately monotonic fashion over cycles and voltage. Thus, the use of non-overlapping kernels can capture the gradual changes in local region while reducing the number of weights in the following layers. A simpler network is more likely to generalize, be computationally faster, and learn from fewer training examples [28].

The ReLU activation function $[ y = \operatorname* { m a x } ( 0 , x ) ]$ was deployed on the batch normalized inputs for all the convolutional layers and the fully connected hidden layer due to its computational simplicity and non-saturating nonlinearities in terms of gradient descent (prevention of vanishing gradients) [26], [29]. The output layer with three neurons uses the sigmoid activation function $[ y = ( 1 + \mathbf { e } ^ { - x } ) ^ { - 1 } ]$ to constrain the predictions to the [0, 1] range of the CDF. We use quantile functions to convert the CDF values to parameter estimates. We then use the estimated parameters along with a rollover width of 50 to predict capacity fade curve using the bilinear equation (Equation (5)). Since the Cauchy quantile function output range lies in -∞ to +∞, we put a constraint on the predicted initial slope to keep its values between -∞ to 0 according to physical meaning of the parameter.

Three different loss functions were investigated: FullCapLoss, 2ndHalfCapLoss, and ParL1Loss (details in Section 5) for training the network. We deploy the Adam optimizer with an exponential learning rate decay scheduler which decays the initial learning rate by a factor of 0.99 every epoch - this process of learning rate scheduling (annealing)

helps to stabilize the network training process and reduce the training and validation losses. Multiple batch sizes were used to randomly divide (during each epoch) the 108 training cells into mini-batches and to train the network for 1000 epochs. From this the model with the lowest loss on the validation data of 35 cells was selected. We evaluate this final model on the test data of 35 cells and report our results in the following section.

## 5. Results and Discussion

We present the prediction results for the selected CNN model in this section and discuss the effects of model hyperparameters on its performance. Batch size and learning rate are two important hyperparameters in neural networks, which can impact the network training speed and convergence. Thus, four different batch sizes (2, 18, 32, and 108) with three different learning rates (0.0001, 0.001, and 0.01) in a full factorial design tuned these hyperparameters. Batch size is defined as the number of training samples that are used to estimate the error gradient before the model weights are updated. A batch size of 2 represents the lowest batch size necessary to work with the 1-D batch normalization layer used in the network. Batch size of 18 divides the training data into 6 equal mini batches. A commonly used batch size of 32, has also been recommended in [30], but results in unequal mini batches of 32, 32, 32, and 12. A batch size of 108 corresponds to the total number of training samples. Validation loss (on validation data) is our metric for selecting the batch size and learning rate, as our final objective is to build a network that can perform (generalize) well on the data that has not been used for its training.

We start by optimizing these two hyperparameters for the FullCapLoss function. It calculates the MAE loss function (FullCapLoss) between the CNN model predicted capacity fade curve and the actual capacity fade curve (at every 10 cycles) to train the network. It is same as the MAE objective function used to obtain the bilinear equation parameters in a differential evolution optimization framework in Section 3. Batch sizes of 18 and 32 resulted in lower validation loss compared to the other two batch sizes (Fig. S2.). We further investigated these two batch sizes with a more granular range of learning rates and then selected 19 different learning rates in the range of 0.0001 and 0.01. A combination of batch size of 18 and the learning rate of 0.0006 resulted in the lowest validation loss of 0.0115. Hence, we used these hyperparameter values to train the CNN model and evaluated the trained model on the test dataset.

We calculated the MAE between CNN predicted and actual capacity fade curves for each cell in the three datasets: training, validation, and test. Top row of histograms in Fig. 4 show these errors for the three datasets. The median values of MAEs for the three (training, validation, and test) datasets are as follows: 0.0115, 0.0108, and 0.0157, respectively. The network performs well on validation and test datasets and exhibits a similar order and range of errors as training data. Excluding one cell in the test data, the MAPE values for capacity fade do not exceed 5% indicating good performance of the model across all the three datasets. The network can generalize well on the test dataset which was never seen during the development, training, and selection process. The highest and lowest MAPEs for the test dataset are 0.4% and 7.7%. The highest MAPEs for training and validation datasets are 2.6% and 5.0%, respectively. Errors and predicted capacity fade plots for the validation data cells were anayzed. While the overall fits were good (≤ 5% MAPE), the loss function value was much higher for the latter half (rapid capacity decay) compared to that for the first half of capacity fade curves (0.0204 vs 0.0026). Thus, another loss function (2ndHalfCapLoss) was explored for training the network, which penalizes for the error (MAE at every 10 cycles) only in the latter half of the capacity fade curve while ignoring the first half. We again explored two batch sizes of 18 and 32 at 19 different learning rates and found the lowest validation loss of 0.0188 for the batch size of 32 and learning rate of 0.008. This validation loss is lower compared to validation loss (0.0204) obtained using the FullCapLoss function over the second half of capacity fade curve. Middle row of histograms in Fig. 4 show the MAE values (on entire capacity fade curve) for the three datasets obtained using the network trained with the 2ndHalfCapLoss function.

For the network trained with 2ndHalfCapLoss function, the median values of MAEs for the three (training, validation, and test) datasets are as follows: 0.0087, 0.0108, and 0.0105, respectively. The highest MAPEs for training, validation, and test datasets are 4.0%, 4.2%, and 3.7% respectively (< 5% for all the datasets). Interestingly, the 2ndHalfCapLoss function reduced the median and maximum errors on the test dataset and the maximum error on the validation dataset compared to FullCapLoss function. While the first half of the capacity fade curve can be approximately linearly interpolated from the capacity fade during the first 100 cycles (input data), it is the second half of the curve which is more difficult to predict. The 2ndHalfCapLoss function puts the emphasis on the second half of the capacity fade curve reducing the overall error.

A third loss function (ParL1Loss) which calculates the L1 loss or MAE between model predicted bilinear equation parameters (initial slope, rollover cycle, and failure slope) and actual values of parameters was also investigated, but did not include rollover width in the loss calculation due to its fixed value of 50. Considering the different ranges and scales of the parameters, CDF values (range 0 to 1) from the parameter distributions (Equation (6)) for error calculation were used to prevent the unequal effects of parameter scales on the loss calculation. A batch size of 32 and learning rate of 0.0002 provided the minimum L1 loss on parameters for the validation data. Using these hyperparameter values, we obtained the following median MAE values for capacity fade curves for the three datasets: 0.0054, 0.0150, and 0.0132 (Fig. 5 bottom row). The median errors for validation and test dataset are higher for this loss function compared to that with 2ndHalfLoss function; thus, a loss function minimizing the error between actual and predicted bilinear equation parameters does not result in a better capacity fade curve fit.

The application of the bilinear equation in describing the capacity fade curves allows us to extract the rollover cycle for each cell. We compare the extracted rollover cycles from the actual capacity fade curves to those predicted by the output layer (after applying quantile function) of the CNN and calculate the actual and predicted EOL for each cell by determining the cycles where the actual (fitted bilinear equation) and predicted capacity fade curves respectively reaches 0.88 Ah value (80% of nominal 1.1 Ah). The parity plots for rollover cycle and EOL for the three datasets are offered in Fig. 6 and 7 corresponding to the three loss functions. These plots show larger errors in rollover cycles and EOL for cells with long-life which is explained by the limited training data (just 8 training data cells beyond 1200 cycles) in that region.

Table 2 lists the MAE and MAPE values corresponding to the rollover cycle and EOL for the three datasets. The errors for EOL are higher compared to those obtained by the CNN model developed by strange and dos Reis [16] (MAPE of 8.8 % when batch 8 is excluded) and by the linear model developed by Severson et al. [15] (MAPE of 9.1%) on the same dataset. However, this higher error on EOL stems from the differences in the modeling approaches and loss functions in the two studies. Strange and dos Reis [16] have used EOL as one of the parameters to define the capacity fade curve and used loss function that directly minimizes the error between predicted vs actual EOL values. Severson et al. [15] also trained their model to specifically reduce error on the EOL. On the other hand, EOL was not used in our bilinear equation and our loss functions of FullCapLoss and 2ndHalfCapLoss focus on the capacity fade curves but not on the individual parameters used to define the curve. When we use the ParL1Loss function which minimizes the error between the predicted vs actual bilinear equation parameters, a reduction of error is observed on both the rollover cycle as well as EOL. Thus, ParL1Loss can be a good choice for the loss function when the objective is to predict individual points like rollover cycle and EOL. These results also highlight the adaptability of the proposed CNN architecture to different loss (objective) functions.

## 6. Conclusions

The advancement of machine learning tools and the increasing availability of battery testing datasets from different institutions have opened a door for advanced battery state of health characterization and prediction. This study utilizes an open access experimental graphite/ $\mathrm { i F e P O _ { 4 } }$ battery dataset (178 cells) and demonstrates the effectiveness of a convolutional neural network architecture in predicting the entire capacity fade degradation curve using just the first 100 cycles of data. The developed modeling approach utilizes a bilinear equation for the capacity fade of batteries, which can describe both linear (without rollover) and bilinear (with rollover) behavior and hence can be easily applied to other battery chemistries not covered in the dataset. Inclusion of convolutional layers in the neural network results in better capacity fade fits and less complex network (model) as compared to a fully connected network.

Our investigation of three different loss functions – FullCapLoss, 2ndHalfCapLoss, and ParL1Loss – reveals that a single convolutional neural network model can be efficiently trained in multiple ways to improve either the overall capacity fade curve fits or the point predictions of rollover cycles and end of life. The loss function (2ndHalfCapLoss) that focuses and minimizes the mean absolute error on the rapid capacity decay part of the curve rather than the error on the entire curve results in the best capacity fade fits among the three loss functions. In a first detailed quantitative analysis of capacity fade trend predictions (on the graphite/LiFePO<sub>4</sub> dataset) using early life data, our designed network performs with a worst-case mean absolute percentage error of 4.0%, 4.2%, and 3.7% for the training (108 cells), validation (35 cells), and test (35 cells) datasets, respectively. The best performance of the network on the test datset was 19% and 17% mean absolute percentage error on the rollover cycle and end of life predictions, respectively, by using a loss function (ParL1Loss) that minimizes the error on the predicted bilinear equation parameters. While the presented modeling approach provides a foundation for early-stage battery degradation characterization to enable rapid deployment, it can be further investigated to track the battery capacity fade curve at any point in battery life (even beyond 80% capacity drop) using the data from the last few cycles. The future work will also include the assessment and prediction of battery health beyond the capacity fade trends such as the prediction of failure mechanisms.
